## 1. Where the project stands (facts, from the code and Days 1–57)

* A rule-based scoring layer (ATS, screening, HR, technical, machine test, aggregation, decision, report), plus governance logic (audit trail, consent,
  retention, access control) and a simulation and stabilization pass. **No language model. No video or camera processing: zero camera/video libraries imported
  anywhere** — a deliberate Day 48–49 decision.
* **Not built:** persistence, login, deployed environment, real speech-to-text (mocked), telephony, any learned model, any enforcement point for consent or retention.
* **Open from Day 57:** a confidently wrong technical answer still scores like a correct paraphrase (O1); cross-stage claims do not reach the decision (O2); the
  machine-test stage is outside the journey (O3). Scoring logic costs tens of milliseconds per candidate (Day 56), so **the cost and latency of scale will sit in
  speech, video and network, not in scoring.**

## 2. The three improvement areas

### 2.1 AI video analysis

| | |
|---|---|
| **What the PRD asks** | Face detection, gaze tracking, multiple-face detection, behaviour and malpractice detection (among others Phases 11–12, 18–19, 33–34, 41–42, 58–59, 66–67, 82–83) |
| **What the project decided** | Days 48–49: no video pipeline, no camera-based gaze or face analysis; integrity from browser events only |
| **What research says** | Biometric analysis of interview video draws consent and notice duties (Illinois AI Video Interview Act; GDPR/DPDP for biometric data) and fairness risk (detection accuracy varies with lighting and skin tone — to be measured, not assumed) |
| **A caution (judgement)** | Gaze and "eye contact" tracking (Phases 12, 41, 66, 83) can disadvantage candidates with disabilities, neurodivergent candidates or different cultural norms; at most a flag for a human, never a score |
| **Recommendation** | Split it. **Do** speech-delivery measures for *candidate practice only* (pace, pauses, filler words, volume: C3). **Gate** integrity video *flags* (face present, one person, camera on: V1) behind consent, a fairness test and a jurisdiction switch, as flags for a human, never a rejection. **Do not** judge expression or traits |
| **Conditions to revisit Day 48** | consent flow live (F4), media pipeline (F5), bias monitoring (F6), jurisdiction switch (S4), and a held-out test showing a low and *equal* false-flag rate across groups |

### 2.2 Emotion detection — recommended against

* **Law.** The EU AI Act (Art. 5(1)(f)) prohibits AI that infers emotions in the workplace, with medical and safety exceptions, since 2 Feb 2025; the Commission's
  guidance reads "workplace" to include recruitment. The guidance is non-binding and counsel decides applicability, but a feature that is prohibited where a customer
  may operate is not one to build into a product.
* **Science.** The main scientific review (Barrett et al., 2019) found that facial movements do not reliably and specifically indicate emotions, which is the premise of the technology.
* **Industry.** HireVue dropped facial analysis from its assessments in 2021 after its own research found it added little.
* **This project.** Days 48–49 already chose not to do it. The PRD asks for affect inference in three phases: 18 (facial expressions, "confidence, stress"), 41 (facial expressions) and 87 ("confidence & stress indicators", "honesty"), and lists "Emotion & confidence analysis" as a future enhancement; **those are the items to amend (D1).** Phases 66 and 83 ask for gaze, focus and distraction tracking, which is the related question in 2.1.
* **What to do instead.** Coverage of answer content, integrity flags for a human, delivery measures for practice. These give recruiters and candidates the useful parts without inferring a state of mind.
* **A related question already inside the code (R1 / D2).** The Day 36 "confidence" and "stress" scores are word-pattern heuristics on transcript text, not biometric, so they are
  likely outside the *biometric* definition the guidance uses, but they carry the label and 25 % of an answer's score. Whether text-only inference counts is a legal question to put to counsel;
  the engineering step is cheap: relabel, measure against human ratings (F3), and keep them out of any rejection rule.

### 2.3 Real-time feedback

| Use | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| During a **live scored interview**, tell candidates how they are doing (V5) | **Not recommended** | It changes the behaviour being measured, leaks the rubric, and treats candidates unequally if some use it more |
| During a live interview, **non-evaluative technical checks** (mic level, framing, connection) (V4) | **Do** | Fewer interviews lost to technical problems; no evaluation involved |
| Inside **practice mode**, the coach responds as the candidate speaks (V6) | **Do, after the coach exists** | The one place immediate feedback helps and fairness is not at stake |

Evidence that practice feedback helps exists but is thin: a randomised trial of 90 university students (MACH, 2013) found those who practised with automated feedback improved on
counsellor ratings and the controls did not. It is small, a week long and old; it supports a pilot, not a promise.

## 3. Proposed features

### 3.1 Resume readiness check (prototype built today)
Tells a candidate, at upload, whether the system can read their resume: sections found, headings it does not read, dates it cannot parse, and skills the job names that the resume does not mention.
*Why:* Day 57 found one resume scoring 14.9 or 50.8 depending only on its skills heading. The demo reproduces it live: identical content scores **50.1** under "Skills:" and "Technical Skills" and **14.2** under "Tools I use", where the check reports
`NO_SKILLS_SECTION` as a blocker. *Design rules, all enforced by tests:* it reports whether the **system can read** the resume; it never returns the score, weights or how to raise a score (coaching against the exact rubric invites gaming);
the only job hint is "add them only if you genuinely have them"; the report holds no resume text; it is stateless and not an input to any decision. 16 of 16 sample resumes pass; awkward ones are flagged with plain-language fixes (`parsers/resume_readiness.py`, 38 tests).

### 3.2 AI coaching system (C2, C3, V6)
Candidates practise with the existing question banks and get qualitative feedback on coverage, structure and clarity, plus speech-delivery measures. **The hard design problem is gaming, not technology:**
a candidate who practises against the exact rubric that later grades them will optimise for the rubric, and Day 57 showed that rubric is phrase-matched. So: **different question variants and rubric from the hiring interview;
qualitative feedback instead of the hiring score; practice data firewalled from recruiters and the hiring decision; opt-in; deletion on request.**

### 3.3 Candidate improvement suggestions (C1 now, C4 later)
C1 above is the first. C4 is a short, **human-reviewed**, category-level explanation after a decision (what went well, where the process found a shortfall against the role), never scores, weights or information about others,
never auto-sent for a rejection. It also serves the explanation duties that data-protection and hiring-AI rules are moving towards.

### 3.4 Interview analytics dashboard (C5, C6)
Funnel by stage, score distributions, the human-override rate, AI-vs-human agreement (from the Day 56 rating worksheet) and the Day 56 cross-stage inconsistency flags, all fed from the audit log. C6 adds aggregate selection-rate gaps and score drift once bias monitoring (F6) exists. This is the feature that makes every
later AI change measurable.

**Two dates that fix the order.** India's DPDP Rules (notified Nov 2025) make the core notice, consent, security and breach duties apply from **mid-May 2027**, so consent wiring (F4) is Phase 1.
EU high-risk obligations for recruitment AI apply from **2 Dec 2027** under the Digital Omnibus, so bias monitoring and documentation (F6) is Phase 2. The US rules named in section 8 apply by where candidates are located, not where Zecpath is.

**Scaling AI has four dimensions, and only one is compute.**
1. *Engineering:* persistence, login, a job queue and workers for speech and video (F1, F5, F7).
2. *Evidence:* an evaluation harness and human ratings (F2, F3) — nothing that learns can be trusted before these exist.
3. *Governance:* consent, bias monitoring, a jurisdiction policy engine (F4, F6, S4), because the law changes yearly and must not be hard-coded into features.
4. *Models:* a learned scorer only in shadow mode and only if it beats the keyword baseline against human ratings with equal error rates across groups (S3, Phase 4).

## 5. Innovation proposal

**Title:** Candidate Readiness and Recruiter Insight.
**Problem.** Candidates can be silently shut out by formatting (Day 57: 14.9 vs 50.8 on the same content), cannot see why a process ended, and have nowhere to practise; recruiters cannot see where the process loses people or whether the AI agrees with them.
**Proposal.** In order: (1) the resume readiness check; (2) the interview analytics dashboard on the audit log; (3) a practice coach with a hard firewall from hiring decisions. All use modules that already exist; none infers emotion.
**Why it is innovative without being risky.** It spends the AI on helping candidates reach a fair evaluation and on making the AI's own behaviour visible, instead of adding another thing that judges people.
**Pilot (readiness check, now).** Run it at upload; measure the share of uploads with no blocker, and the share of candidates who fix a blocker after seeing it. Success is a candidate-side improvement in readable resumes; it must not change any scoring.
**Metrics for later features.** Coach: improvement on an independent rater's scale versus a control (the MACH design). Dashboard: recruiters answer "where do we lose candidates and does the AI agree with us?" without a developer.
**Guardrails.** No emotion or trait inference; no score or rubric leakage; practice data never reaches recruiters; every candidate-facing message about a rejection is reviewed by a person.
**Resources and cost.** Not estimated: no headcount or budget is known, and inventing figures would mislead.
**Decision requested:** D1–D5 above.

## 6. Future architecture ideas

Today the system is a set of stateless scoring functions behind a thin API. A target shape that keeps what works and adds what is missing:

| Layer | Today | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| Edge / media | mocked speech-to-text; no video | streaming STT/TTS and telephony behind a **job queue with autoscaled workers**; media never in the request path (F5, S1) |
| Orchestration | each stage called by a script | an **event-driven workflow** (`resume.uploaded → ats.scored → call.completed → hr.scored → …`), idempotent handlers, a first-class **human review queue** at every point a person must act |
| Scoring services | stateless engines (59 modules) | unchanged and horizontally scalable; **every score carries model and config version** (already in the audit log); new scorers run in **shadow mode** beside the old one |
| Decision | recommendation engine, never auto-reject | unchanged; add the cross-stage inconsistency flags (Day 56) as review reasons |
| Governance plane (cross-cutting) | consent, retention, audit, access logic (Day 55), not enforced | enforced at the gateway; **jurisdiction policy engine** with per-feature kill switches (S4); the audit log as the event store |
| Data plane | none | relational store + encrypted object store; identity separated from content; **practice data in a separate store, firewalled from hiring** |
| Evaluation plane | edge sweep + simulation scripts | the same checks in CI, a **model registry**, a human-label store, drift and fairness monitors (F2, F3, C6) |

Principles: (1) the human decides, the system recommends; (2) every AI feature has an off switch per jurisdiction; (3) a feature that needs data we do not have waits for the data; (4) candidate-facing tools never share a rubric with hiring tools;
(5) nothing is promoted without agreement with human ratings; (6) architecture follows the cost centre, which is media, not scoring.

**What we would not build:** emotion or honesty inference; real-time scoring feedback in a scored interview; a hidden second profile of candidates from practice data.

## 7. Risks

| Risk | Level (judgement) | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| A feature turns out to be prohibited or restricted where a customer operates | High | jurisdiction policy engine (S4); legal review at each phase gate; nothing affect-inferring scheduled (enforced by `validate_backlog`) |
| The coach teaches candidates to game the rubric | High | separate question variants and rubric; qualitative feedback; firewall |
| Consent and rights are designed but not wired before the May 2027 date | High | F4 in Phase 1 |
| Learned scoring adds bias that rules did not | High | shadow mode; subgroup error rates; promotion gate (S3) |
| Estimates (value / effort / feasibility) are wrong | Medium | they are labelled judgement; re-score at each gate; the validator checks consistency, not worth |
| Practice data leaks into hiring decisions | Medium | separate store, access rules, tests |
| Over-building before the foundation (persistence, login) exists | Medium | dependency order enforced by `build_order` |

## 9. Limits

* **Scores are judgement.** Value, effort and feasibility are my 1–5 estimates; `priority_score = 2·value + feasibility − effort` sorts them, it does not forecast anything.
* **No dates, costs or headcount.** Only the regulators' dates are used; effort is relative.
* **Not legal advice**, and the law was read through secondary sources.
* **One prototype.** Only the readiness check is built; the coach, dashboard and everything else are proposals. The readiness prototype was tested on 16 clean sample resumes and a few constructed awkward ones, not on real uploads.
* **The evidence for coaching is one small, old trial**; the proposal asks for a controlled pilot before any claim of benefit.
* **The validator checks consistency** (dependencies, gates, paths), not whether a feature is a good idea.
